Florence Between Northern and Southern Italy

The question

What is the relationship between Florence’s seasonal climate pattern and the north–south distribution of temperature and rainfall across Italy?

The data

I used the ArcGIS Living Atlas layer World Historical Climate — Monthly Averages for GHCN-D Stations for 1981–2010, published by Esri using NOAA NCEI GHCN-Daily data. I accessed the layer on September 13, 2026. The dataset provides thirty-year monthly averages of temperature and precipitation using 1981–2010 as the reference period. This is not the current WMO climate normal, which is 1991–2020. The layer description and the live table also report different total station counts, showing that the metadata and current dataset are not fully synchronized.

The method

I first filtered the global station layer using GHCN-D ID starts with IT, which returned 43 stations in Italy. I then selected the station nearest to Florence, located in Pisa, and added a second filter: GHCN-D ID is ITM00016158. The two expressions were joined with AND so that only one station remained. I created two bar charts using the twelve monthly average temperature fields in °C and the twelve monthly mean precipitation fields in mm, with aggregation set to Mean. I also examined several northern Italian lowland stations and one southern station in Sicily to compare the regional north–south pattern.

What I found

Florence shows a Mediterranean climate pattern: July and August are the hottest months at about 23.73°C, January is the coldest at about 6.37°C, November is the wettest month, and July is the driest. Rainfall is therefore concentrated more in the cooler season rather than the hottest part of the year. This places Florence between the cooler northern Italian lowlands and the warmer, more strongly Mediterranean south. Northern stations such as Ghedi and Verona have colder winters and more evenly distributed rainfall, while southern Sicily has hotter summers and a much clearer dry-summer pattern. Florence is not an extreme case, but an intermediate one within Italy’s broader north–south climate distribution. At a larger scale, this regional relationship also fits the wider pattern of warmer low latitudes and drier subtropical zones shaped in part by atmospheric circulation.

What this does not show

The main limitation is that the nearest station is in Pisa, so I am using one station outside Florence to stand for Florence’s local climate. That assumption may be affected by differences in location, elevation, and local conditions. The wider map also has uneven station coverage, with much denser observations in places such as Europe and North America than in central Africa, deserts, oceans, and Antarctica, so blank areas should not be read as evidence of uniform climate. The dataset also uses the 1981–2010 climate normal rather than the current 1991–2020 WMO normal. The baseline exercise showed why baseline matter: the same tropical temperature of 26.13°C gave an anomaly of +0.95°C against 1991–2020 but +1.18°C against 1979–2000.
